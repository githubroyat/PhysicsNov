Topic 1 of 6 · your notes, 1–2 Oct

Hooke's law & the spring constant

In short

  • WHATA spring pulls back with a force that grows in proportion to how far you stretch or squash it: F = −k·x.
  • HOWk = F / x, in N/m. Read k as the slope of an F–x graph. Convert cm to m before you calculate.
  • WHYThe minus sign says the spring force points opposite to the displacement: it always pulls back towards the rest position.

Hooke's law

F = −k·xF = force of the spring (N) · x = how far the spring is stretched or compressed from its original length (m) · k = spring constant (N/m)

The minus sign shows the direction: the spring force always points opposite to the displacement. It is a restoring force, pulling back toward the rest position. Stretch the spring down, the force points up.

When you only want the size of the force, drop the minus sign:

F = k·x k = Fx x = Fk

Unit of k

k is measured in N/m. Because 1 N = 1 kg·m/s², we get 1 N/m = 1 kg/s². Your notes wrote kg/s² and that is correct.

What does k mean?

k is the force you need per metre of stretch. A big k is a stiff spring (hard to stretch). A small k is a soft spring.

The elastic region

Elastic region (Hooke's law region): the range in which force and extension are proportional and the spring returns to its original length when released. The end of this range is the elastic limit.

Reading k from an F–x graph

On an F–x graph the straight line has slope k = F / x. A steeper line means a bigger k, so a stiffer spring.

Topic 2 of 6 · your notes, 2 Oct and 15 Oct

Describing oscillations

In short

  • WHATy(t) is where the object is at time t. r is the biggest elongation (the amplitude), T is one full oscillation, f = 1/T is how many per second.
  • HOWy(t) = r·cos(ω·t) with ω = 2π/T = 2π·f. Put your calculator in RAD mode.
  • WHYOne full oscillation matches one full turn of 2π rad, which takes T seconds. That is why a point on a circle draws the sine curve.

The words

Picture a weight bouncing up and down on a spring.

  • Elongation y(t) (or x(t)): the displacement from the rest position at time t, in m. In your words: "where it is".
  • Amplitude r: the maximum elongation, in m. (Other books write ŷ or A. Your class uses r.)
  • Period T: the time for one full oscillation, there and back (for example top → bottom → top). Unit s.
  • Frequency f: the number of full oscillations per second. Unit hertz: 1 Hz = 1/s = s−1.
f = 1T T = 1f

Angular velocity ω

ω = 2πT = 2π·f unit: rad/s (also written 1/s)

In your class ω is called angular velocity; some books call it angular frequency. It tells you how much angle (in radians) is covered per second. One full oscillation is one full turn = 2π rad, and it takes T seconds. That is the whole idea of ω = 2π / T.

Check your notes

Your first attempt was r·cos(t). It is missing ω. The full form is r·cos(ω·t). The thing inside the cosine is an angle: ω·t.

sin or cos? It does matter

  • y = r·sin(ω·t) starts at y = 0 (the rest position) at t = 0.
  • y = r·cos(ω·t) starts at y = r (the maximum) at t = 0.

Same shape, shifted by a quarter period (T/4): the sine curve reaches its maximum T/4 later than the cosine curve. Choose the one that matches where the object is at t = 0.

The unit circle

In the class simulation a point moves around a circle of radius r at constant angular velocity ω. After time t it has turned through the angle α = ω·t. Its height comes from a right-angled triangle: sin α = opposite / hypotenuse = y / r, so y = r·sin(α). Plot that height against time and you get the sine curve of the oscillation.

Trig reminder (SOH CAH TOA): sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse · cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse · tan θ = opposite/adjacent.

Damping

In short

  • WHATDamping is friction (air resistance, friction inside the spring) taking energy away, so the bouncing weight slowly comes to rest.
  • HOWThe amplitude shrinks by about the same factor every half-swing: An = A0·0.8n. The period T stays the same.
  • WHYFriction removes energy, so the weight reaches less far each time. It does not change how long one swing takes (for the weak damping seen in class).

What damping does

Damping: different kinds of friction take energy away from the oscillation, so the amplitude gradually gets smaller until the weight is at rest.
  • The amplitude goes down.
  • The frequency (and the period) stays the same. The oscillation just gets smaller.

Check your notes

The word is damping, not "dampening". Your answers are accepted either way here, but use "damping" in the test.

Shock absorbers in a car are your example: they damp the bouncing of the car on its springs, so the car stops bouncing quickly. Other examples Extra: a swing slowing down, a guitar string fading out, a door closer.

Pendulums

In short

  • WHATA pendulum is a weight swinging on a string. How fast it swings depends on its length l and on g, but not on its mass.
  • HOWω = √(g/l), T = 2π·√(l/g), g = 4π²·l/T². Valid for small swing angles.
  • WHYOnly g and l appear in ω = √(g/l). Mass is not in the formula. Longer pendulum: slower. Stronger gravity: faster.

What does the frequency depend on?

Does the frequency depend on …AnswerReason
m (mass)Nom does not appear in ω = √(g/l).
l (length)Yesl is in the formula: the shorter the pendulum, the faster it swings (higher frequency).
g (gravitational acceleration)Yesg is in the formula: the stronger the gravity, the higher the frequency.

The formulas

ω = √gl ω = 2π·f f = 1T

Put ω = 2π / T and ω = √(g/l) together:

Derivation (2 lines) Extra · worked out from your formulas

2πT = √gl  ⟹  T = 2π·√lg

Square ω = √(g/l): ω² = g/l  ⟹  g = ω²·l, and with ω = 2π/T: g = 4π²·lT²

T = 2π·√lg g = ω²·l = 4π²·lT²

Consequence: Extra · worked out from your formulas 4 times longer means the period doubles (T grows with the square root of l).

Small angles only. Extra These formulas are an approximation for small swing angles, roughly below 10–15°.

Topic 5 of 6 · your notes, 6 Nov

Resonance

In short

  • WHATA repeating (periodic) force acts on a system that can oscillate, at or close to its natural frequency. The amplitude gets larger, even very large.
  • HOWCompare the driving frequency with the natural frequency. Equal or close: big amplitude. Far below: the mass just follows the driver (about ×1). Far above: it hardly moves. More damping: lower peak.
  • WHYThink of pushing a swing: pushes in rhythm with the swing build up the motion.

What resonance is

Resonance happens when an external (periodic) force is applied to a system that can oscillate, at or close to the natural frequency of that system. The amplitude increases (it can become very large).
In the words of your notes: an external force acts on a potentially oscillating system at or close to its natural (or "individual") frequency.

The natural frequency (also called eigenfrequency Extra) is the frequency at which a system oscillates by itself after it was disturbed once.

What happens to the amplitude?

  • Far below the natural frequency f0 the mass just follows the driver (the amplitude is about ×1 the driver's movement).
  • Far above f0 the mass hardly moves.
  • Near f0 the amplitude is the largest.
  • Extra Strong damping keeps the resonance peak lower (and the peak then sits slightly below f0).

Examples

Examples for your answers (not from your notes)

  • Pushing a child on a swing in rhythm with the swing.
  • A wine glass shattered by a loud sound at its natural frequency.
  • Parts of a car or bus rattling at one particular engine speed.
  • Soldiers are ordered to break step when marching over a bridge.
  • The London Millennium Bridge (2000) wobbled sideways when the crowd's footsteps matched its natural frequency. It was fixed by adding dampers.

Topic 6 of 6 · your notes, mid Nov

Waves

In short

  • WHATOscillations that spread out in space are waves. The particles only oscillate around their rest position. What travels is the oscillation (the disturbance, the energy).
  • HOWc = f·λ. Transverse: oscillation ⟂ direction of travel. Longitudinal: oscillation ∥ direction of travel.
  • WHYEach particle is set oscillating by its neighbour. The speed depends on the medium (for a string: the tension), not on frequency or amplitude.

What is a wave?

Waves are oscillations that spread out in space. Each particle is set oscillating (excited) by its neighbour, so the oscillation is passed along.

In your notes: oscillation ≙ excitation. Each particle is excited by its neighbour. The particles only oscillate around their rest position. They do not travel with the wave. What travels is the oscillation (the disturbance, the energy).

Transverse and longitudinal waves

Transverse wave

The direction of oscillation is perpendicular to the direction the wave travels. (Also called transversal.)

  • A rope moved up and down: the wave travels horizontally.
  • A guitar string. Extra
  • Extra The stadium wave ("La Ola"): people stand up and sit down, the wave runs around the stadium.

Longitudinal wave

The direction of oscillation is parallel to the direction the wave travels.

  • Sound in air (compressions and rarefactions Extra).
  • A slinky pushed and pulled along its length. Extra
  • People in a queue being pushed.

Check your notes

You wrote "longitude wave". The word is longitudinal. "Longitude" is the geography word (lines on a map). Water waves are a mix of both types, so do not use them as an example of either.

Wavelength, crest, trough, amplitude

  • Wavelength λ: the distance between two neighbouring crests (or two neighbouring troughs; for longitudinal waves two neighbouring compressions). Unit m.
  • Crest = highest point. Trough = lowest point.
  • Amplitude = maximum displacement from the rest position.

Wave speed

c = f · λ c = λTc = wave speed (m/s) · f = frequency (Hz) · λ = wavelength (m) · T = period (s)

In one period the wave moves on by one wavelength, so c = λ / T Extra. Since f = 1/T, that is the same as c = f·λ.

Worked example (your control question)

What does the speed depend on?

  • Yes: the medium. For a string, the tension: higher tension means faster.
  • No: the frequency, and not the amplitude.

Same medium, double the frequency: the wavelength halves and the speed stays the same.

Reflection at the end of a string

  • Fixed end: the pulse comes back upside down (inverted). In your words: it "goes under".
  • Loose (free) end: the pulse comes back the same way up (upright).

If the returning pulse looks smaller, that is energy loss (damping), not the rule.

Top traps in tests

  • cm → m. Convert before you calculate. 1.7 cm = 0.017 m, not 1.7 m.
  • RAD mode. y = r·cos(ω·t) needs the calculator in RAD, not DEG.
  • Half period vs period. Top → bottom is only half a period. T is there and back.
  • T vs f. f = 1/T. If T = 0.82 s, then f = 1/0.82 = 1.22 Hz. Do not divide the wrong way round.
  • Forgetting 2π. ω = 2π/T. Not 1/T, not π/T.
  • The minus sign in Hooke's law. F = −k·x shows direction; for sizes use F = k·x.
  • Mass does not change a pendulum's period. Only l and g do.
  • Amplitude does not change the wave speed or the period. Frequency does not change the wave speed either. Tension does.
  • With damping the frequency stays the same. Only the amplitude shrinks.
